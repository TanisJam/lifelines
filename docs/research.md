# Research notes

Prior art that shaped Lifelines. Each section ends with **what we take from it**. Decisions live in [decisions.md](./decisions.md).

## Dwarf Fortress

- World generation is "a giant zero-player strategy game … and history is just a record of that" (Tarn Adams, [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-dwarf-fortress-i-figuring-out-how-to-simulate-the-universe-one-step-at-a-time)).
- Legends mode renders prose from structured data: `historical_events`, `historical_event_collections` (wars, battles, duels), entities, sites and artifacts ([XML dump](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:XML_dump)).
- The richness comes from simple interacting rules (personality, values, grudges, relationships), not authored plots ([Adams, *Emergent Narrative in DF*](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.1201/9780429488337-15/emergent-narrative-dwarf-fortress-tarn-adams)).
- Limits: histories become repetitive, and players supply much of the story themselves (apophenia).

**What we take from it:** simulate first and describe afterwards; a few rules that interact; surface what matters, so readers don't have to dig through the volume.

## Dwarf Fortress inner life

DF's characters feel alive because of a layered psychological model. Where a source was unclear, the table says so.

| System | How it works | Source |
|--------|--------------|--------|
| Facets | About 50 personality facets on a 0–100 scale, rendered in 7 text bands (40–60 is neutral and produces no text). Example: ANGER 91–100 → "is in a constant state of internal rage" | [Personality facet](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Personality_facet) |
| Values | About 33 beliefs on a −50…+50 scale. Large value differences between two people breed grudges; a conflict between a value and a facet produces inner-conflict text | [Personality value](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Personality_value) |
| Goals | Dreams such as START_A_FAMILY or CRAFT_A_MASTERWORK. Fulfilling one gives a happy thought and the text "…and this dream was realized" | [Personality goal](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Personality_goal) |
| Needs | About 24 needs, each weighted by personality. Unmet needs cause stress and reduce focus | [Need](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Need) |
| Thoughts & stress | Events produce emotion-tagged thoughts, with intensity scaled by facets. Stress accumulates, and the breakdown type follows the dominant propensity: anger leads to a tantrum, anxiety to obliviousness, depression to melancholy. Exact thresholds are uncertain | [Emotion](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Emotion), [Insanity](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Insanity) |
| Memory | Short-term memories are promoted to long-term; about 1 in 3 long-term memories become core, and a core memory **permanently changes a facet** | [Memory](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Memory_(thought)) |
| Relationships | Repeated chats build rank: friend, then close friend, then kindred spirit; or a grudge on the negative side. Compatibility sets the direction. Courtship is gated by love propensity and age gap. Thresholds are uncertain | [Relationship](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Relationship), [Marriage](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Marriage) |
| Text | "He felt satisfied upon improving mining." Revisiting a memory changes the phrasing: "…remembering…" | [Thought](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Thought) |

## Story sifting

Story sifting means finding narratable patterns in a simulation's log.

- **Felt** ([paper](https://mkremins.github.io/publications/Felt_SimpleStorySifter.pdf), [code](https://github.com/mkremins/felt)): declarative sifting patterns queried over the event log.
- **Winnow** ([paper](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/18903/18903-52-22669-1-2-20211004.pdf)): incremental matching while events stream in.
- **James Ryan**: *Curating Simulated Storyworlds* ([thesis](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1340j5h2)); [Talk of the Town](https://github.com/james-owen-ryan/talktown) (a 200-year town sim with fallible memory); Sheldon County / Hennepin.

**What we take from it:** phase 2. Highlight cascades automatically after a fork ("because X didn't marry Y, Z was never born").

## Similar games and generators

- **Caves of Qud**: generates events first and rationalizes causality afterwards with grammars ([paper](https://www.freeholdgames.com/papers/Generation_of_mythic_biographies_in_Cavesofqud.pdf)). It is cheaper, but its causality is fake; we keep real simulation.
- **RimWorld storytellers**: a pacing layer on top of the sim ([wiki](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/AI_Storytellers)).
- **Wildermyth**: procedural beats rendered as comic panels, which makes randomness feel authored.
- **Crusader Kings 3**: event cards that show each option's consequences, the template for our link inspector.
- **Legends viewers** ([LegendsViewer-Next](https://github.com/Kromtec/LegendsViewer-Next)): a cross-linked wiki built from the event export.
- **Generative Agents** ([paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.03442)): memory scored by recency, importance and relevance; the model is called only when importance crosses a threshold, which keeps cost bounded.

## Life by social class, 1498–1558

What village life looked like for each social class between about 1498 and 1558, at the end of the Middle Ages and the start of the early modern period. It covers peasants and serfs, artisans, merchants, clergy, and the nobility and gentry. This is the historical baseline the engine is checked against in [Engine cross-check](#engine-cross-check-1498-1558). Decisions are recorded in [decisions.md](./decisions.md) only after they are agreed.

**How to read this section**

- **Every figure has four parts:** the value, its range or caveat, the population and period it actually describes, and the source. The *Where & when* column says when a figure is a **proxy** from outside 1498–1558. Most are: English parish registers begin in 1538, national aggregates in 1541, family reconstitution in 1580, and the best peasant evidence (manorial court rolls) ends around 1400.
- **Some figures are marked UNVERIFIED.** No primary or peer-reviewed secondary source could confirm them. They are kept only so the gap is visible, and they must not be hard-coded.
- **England is the anchor.** It is the only region with continuous quantitative data for the window. Continental contrasts are noted where they matter: the Hajnal line, the "second serfdom" east of the Elbe, the German Peasants' War, and the Florentine Catasto.
- **Source quality varies.** Monographs and journal articles are primary anchors. Wikipedia and teaching sites appear only as pointers to the scholarly work they summarize, and they are labelled that way.
- **The figures were fact-checked** by an independent pass over the claims most likely to be wrong: Cummins 2017, Wrigley & Schofield, Schofield 1986, the Dissolution and chantry counts, Alfani's 1524–25 subsidy share, the Hajnal and Laslett figures, and the rebellion and debasement figures. Two citations were corrected, and the changes are listed in each subsection's *Verification log*.

### Mortality and life expectancy

**Scope note on evidence quality.** No source gives a clean, class-disaggregated life table for an English or continental village specifically for 1498–1558. The window sits between the end of the manorial court-roll record (best peasant demographic evidence, c. 1250–1400) and the start of continuous English parish-register aggregate analysis (Wrigley & Schofield's series begins in 1541, with family-reconstitution detail from 1580). Consequently every figure below is either (a) a national/regional aggregate that includes but does not isolate a social class, (b) an elite-only dataset (monks, peerage, gentry) used by historians as a proxy for "privileged" mortality, or (c) drawn from adjacent periods (14th–15th century manorial/monastic data, or post-1580 family reconstitution) and explicitly flagged as a proxy. This is stated plainly in the tables via the "Where & when" column.

#### 8. Seasonality / age pattern of deaths

No source located in this research session gave a seasonality-of-death breakdown specific to 1498–1558. The only seasonal pattern confirmed in the literature is for plague generally: "the characteristic autumn spike associated with plague continued into the early 1700s" (Cummins, Kelly & Ó Gráda, on London plague 1560–1665 — outside our window but describing a pattern the authors treat as long-standing). Sweating sickness is separately noted to have "usually [begun] in the summer and subsided in the fall." No age-specific (as opposed to disease-specific) seasonal mortality pattern was verified for the window. This is a gap.

#### Synthesis for a simulation

The figures below are **best estimates for parameterizing a simulation**, each carrying the same caveats as their source row above. Where no class-specific figure exists, the national aggregate or nearest documented proxy is used and marked as such — do not read these as precise, class-resolved historical facts.

- **National baseline, e0 (all classes, England, ambient/non-crisis years):** ~35–38 years, with strong year-to-year volatility (documented range across quinquennia: ~28–42 years). *Basis: Wrigley & Schofield (1981), applies from 1541 onward; extend backward to 1498 only as an assumption, since no data exist for 1498–1540.*
- **Peasant/customary tenant/copyholder e0:** no direct 16th-century figure; use the national aggregate above as the default (peasants were the demographic majority driving that aggregate), with a plausible downward adjustment (perhaps −2 to −5 years relative to the aggregate) for landless labourers versus tenant families, based on the general historiographical expectation of a status gradient — **this adjustment is an assumption, not a sourced figure**.
- **True serf (east-of-Elbe "second serfdom") e0:** no quantified figure found at all; if the simulation needs a value, treat it as equal to or somewhat worse than the West-European peasant baseline, flagged explicitly as an unsourced assumption.
- **Artisan/urban tradesman e0:** no separate figure found; urban populations are generally expected (per the general historiography of pre-modern urban mortality) to have somewhat higher mortality than rural populations due to density and sanitation, but no 1498–1558 English or continental number was verified — treat any urban penalty as an assumption.
- **Clergy (regular, i.e. monks) e21 (life expectancy conditional on reaching adulthood):** ~+43 years from age 21 (i.e., to about age 64) is the figure most consistently associated with this literature (Hollingsworth/Hatcher comparison), but it derives from 14th–15th century Canterbury/Durham data, not 1498–1558 directly. Use with a wide error bar and treat as an upper-bound "well-provisioned adult male" proxy rather than a clergy-specific effect.
- **Nobility/gentry e0 or age-at-death:** pan-European noble age-at-death rose from ~48 years (pre-1400 cohort) to ~54 years (1400–1650 cohort); this is conditional on adult survivorship, not e0. The most usable class-specific, time-bounded finding is the **violent-death share**: ~30% of adult male noble deaths were battle deaths before 1550, dropping to <5% after 1550 — a strong, sourced signal that noble male mortality risk from warfare fell sharply right across the end of this window.
- **Infant mortality rate (IMR), all classes, rural England:** ~130–175 per 1,000 live births is the best available range, though it is drawn mostly from post-1538 parish-register data and the true 1498–1537 rate is unmeasured; use ~150/1,000 as a working central estimate with a wide (±40/1,000) band.
- **Maternal mortality per birth:** ~9–10 per 1,000 births (0.9–1.0%) as an Elizabethan (post-1558) anchor point; lifetime risk across a reproductive career ~6–7%. Apply to the whole window with low confidence, since the specific figure is not from before 1558.
- **Epidemic shock parameters:** model plague as a recurring but irregular shock (documented English severe-outbreak mortality on the order of 10–12% of an affected population in a bad year, with occasional local catastrophes far higher); model sweating sickness as four additional shock events within the window (1508, 1517, 1528, 1551) that atypically hit adult males and the better-off/clergy harder than the very poor; model the 1557–59 influenza pandemic as a severe, England-wide terminal-window shock (~2% national population loss, with some localities such as Colyton and Worcester showing 3.5–4× normal burial/will rates); model 1555–57 as a compounded dearth-plus-disease crisis with elevated but unquantified excess mortality.
- **War mortality:** treat the 1524–25 Peasants' War as a peasant/artisan-class-specific mortality event (80,000–100,000 dead, overwhelmingly non-noble) localized to the German-speaking territories; treat the Italian Wars (1494–1559) as a chronic, low-background mercenary/urban-siege mortality drag with no reliable aggregate total, disproportionately affecting soldiers and besieged townspeople rather than the rural peasantry directly.

### Marriage, family and inheritance

**Scope note on the evidence base.** Almost none of the classic quantitative demography for pre-industrial Europe is directly reconstructed for the exact window 1498–1558. The two anchor English data sets — Wrigley & Schofield's aggregative reconstruction and the Cambridge Group's family-reconstitution study — begin in 1538–1541 and 1580 respectively, so anything earlier than that is either a Continental parallel (Tuscany 1427, the Low Countries "from the late 1400s"), a medieval manorial-court reconstruction (Halesowen 1270–1400), or a later-16th/17th-century figure used as the nearest available proxy and flagged as such below. Class-disaggregated data (peasant vs artisan vs merchant vs noble) exists in reasonable depth only for (a) English peasants via manorial court rolls, (b) Florentine urban elites via the 1427 Catasto, and (c) the higher nobility via genealogical reconstruction (Hollingsworth). Landless labourers, ordinary artisans, and parish clergy are comparatively invisible in this literature before parish-register-based reconstitution begins (mid-16th century onward in a handful of English/French/German parishes). These gaps are noted explicitly in the tables rather than filled by inference.

#### Marriage rules (for simulation use)

The following rules were in force, as stated, across the 1498–1558 window unless otherwise noted. Each is written as a discrete, simulation-usable condition.

1. **Minimum age of consent (canon law):** girls may be betrothed/married from age 12, boys from age 14, distinguishing betrothal (*sponsalia*) from consummated marriage. In force throughout Catholic Europe for the entire window. *Widely cited across canon-law literature; specific primary citation to Gratian's Decretum or R.H. Helmholz was not independently re-fetched in this pass — treat the age thresholds themselves as well-established but the exact canonical citation as unverified in this session.*
2. **Consanguinity/affinity impediment:** marriage prohibited between persons related within the 4th degree of consanguinity or affinity, per canon 50 of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215). Dispensations from a bishop or higher authority could lift this bar. In force throughout the window (Trent did not change this rule; it only tightened the *form* of marriage in 1563, after our window). Source: Fourth Lateran Council canon 50 (1215); general canon-law overviews (e.g., Catholic Encyclopedia articles on consanguinity/affinity).
3. **Publicity requirement (banns):** the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) also banned secretly contracted marriages and pushed toward public announcement to allow consanguinity impediments to surface; "banns" (public announcement before the wedding, typically on successive feast days) became customary practice well before Trent formalized the mechanism in 1563. Usable in simulation as a soft social norm during 1498–1558, not yet a strict legal precondition for validity.
4. **Clandestine marriage remained canonically valid throughout 1498–1558.** A marriage made by free mutual consent *per verba de praesenti* (words of present consent), even without a priest, witnesses, or banns, was a true and binding sacramental marriage under canon law until the Tametsi decree of the Council of Trent (24th session, 11 November 1563) — which lies **after** our window and should not be applied to it. For simulation purposes: throughout 1498–1558, informal/unwitnessed marriage vows were legally binding (though pastorally discouraged and hard to prove), a major source of clandestine-marriage litigation. Source: [Tametsi – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tametsi); Catholic Encyclopedia, "Clandestinity" (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04001a.htm).
5. **Parental consent was not required for canonical validity** of a marriage between two people old enough to consent, but was very often required or strongly enforced by secular law, manorial custom, guild rule, or family/social pressure — especially for anyone with property, a tenancy, or a guild position to transmit. Treat as: legally optional, socially/economically close to mandatory for propertied classes.
6. **Merchet (manorial marriage fine):** a fine payable to the manorial lord when an unfree tenant's son or (especially) daughter married, particularly if marrying off the manor. In England, this obligation had become rare/marginal ("dwindled to irrelevance") by the mid-15th century and should generally be treated as **absent or vestigial** for English peasants by 1498. Source: [Merchet – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchet); *The Decline of Serfdom in Late Medieval England* (Cambridge UP).
7. **East-Elbian serfdom and marriage control:** in the Gutsherrschaft regions east of the Elbe, serfs required their lord's consent to marry (and had restricted mobility/out-marriage rights). Important timing caveat for simulation: this "second serfdom" regime was only beginning to intensify **during** the 16th century — it was not yet the fully hardened institution of the 17th–18th centuries. For 1498–1558, model East-Elbian lord's-consent-to-marry as an emerging and regionally uneven constraint, growing stronger toward the end of the window, rather than a uniform fixed rule. Source: overview literature on Gutsherrschaft. **Correction:** the cited article is Edgar Melton, "Gutsherrschaft in East Elbian Germany and Livonia, 1500–1800: A Critique of the Model," *Central European History* 21:4 (1988), pp. 315–349 — not W.W. Hagen as originally stated (Hagen has separately published related East-Elbian scholarship, e.g. "Village Life in East-Elbian Germany and Poland, 1400–1800," but is not the author of this specific article; the two were conflated in the original draft). The article-abstract URL without a hash suffix 404s; the working URL is https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/central-european-history/article/abs/gutsherrschaft-in-east-elbian-germany-and-livonia-15001800-a-critique-of-the-model/E75E65E9C9B7B67DE3D56CCAC7CCDCE1 (verified HTTP 200).
8. **Guild rule — marriage as a path into mastership:** in many German (and other Continental) guild towns, a man could gain guild membership/mastership by marrying a master's widow or daughter, effectively inheriting access to the trade through marriage rather than through the standard apprentice→journeyman→master route. Source: review article, "Women, Gender, and Guilds in Early Modern Europe," *International Review of Social History* (Cambridge Core, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/women-gender-and-guilds-in-early-modern-europe-an-overview-of-recent-research/8DD0903AB406FDC4778EC779182053EA); S. Ogilvie, *A Bitter Living: Women, Markets, and Social Capital in Early Modern Germany* (Oxford UP, 2003).
9. **Guild rule — widow's right to continue the workshop, with tightening restrictions:** early guild statutes let a master's widow freely continue the business; from around the mid-15th century onward, most crafts added restrictions — commonly a widow could run the shop for a limited time only (ranging in some German cities to as little as a few months, elsewhere one to two years), might be barred from remarrying outside the trade without losing the privilege, and might need a son to eventually take over. For simulation purposes, treat "widow keeps the shop" as a time-limited, conditional right that narrows over the 15th–16th centuries rather than an unconditional entitlement. Source: same as rule 8, and blog.lostartpress.com, "Craftswomen and the Guilds" (secondary/journalistic level source — treat the specific "few months to two years" figure as indicative rather than precisely verified).
10. **Widow remarriage:** legally free to remarry at any time (no canonical waiting period located in this research). Empirically, remarriage rates for widows were markedly lower than for widowers, and widowers remarried both more often and faster than widows. Source: general early-modern-England widowhood historiography (e.g., material summarized around Folger Shakespeare Library's "How to be a true widow in early modern England," https://www.folger.edu/blogs/collation/how-to-be-a-true-widow/, and Cambridge Core's "London widowhood revisited" — note the latter's period, 17th–early 18th century, is after our window and used only as directional evidence).
11. **Noble/gentry marriage and wardship:** in England, a feudal tenant-in-chief's heir who inherited as a minor became a ward of the Crown (or the Crown's grantee), who thereby also acquired the right to arrange (or sell the right to arrange) that ward's marriage — a mechanism with no equivalent constraint on commoners. This is a well-established feature of English feudal law continuing through the Tudor period (the Court of Wards and Liveries was formally established in 1540, squarely inside our window). Source: general constitutional/legal history of the Court of Wards; not independently re-fetched as a primary citation in this pass — **flagged as well-attested general knowledge rather than freshly verified**.

Design notes:
- Model serfdom as a binary regional flag rather than a graded status: **absent/nominal** in England by 1500 (copyhold, not personal bondage); **variable/incomplete** in France (regional survivals only, unverified national %); **intensifying** in Poland/Bohemia/eastern Germany across the window (early phase of "second serfdom," escalating toward and beyond 1558).
- Model the Dissolution (1536–41) and its resale wave (1540s–50s) as a one-time land-market shock that disproportionately benefits gentry and merchant classes' mobility odds within the window.
- Model literacy as sex-differentiated (women’s illiteracy roughly 90% across the whole 16th–17th-century span per Cressy) and treat all specific numeric literacy rates for exactly 1498–1558 as low-confidence interpolations between Cressy's later-16th-century signature data and earlier qualitative estimates.
- Model tax/dues burden as multiplicative and cumulative for tenants (tithe + rent + heriot/entry fine + royal subsidy when liable), which is why extraordinary levies like the 1525 Amicable Grant provoked revolt — they stacked on top of an already-layered burden.

### Clergy, nobility and period events

*Focus: England, with continental comparanda noted explicitly. All figures carry value, caveat, scope, and citation. Items that could not be independently verified against a primary or peer-reviewed secondary source are marked UNVERIFIED and should be used cautiously, if at all, in a simulation.*

#### Clergy

**Size of the clerical estate.** For the whole religious population of England, Wales, and (nominally) Ireland at the point of the Dissolution, one synthesis (drawing on Knowles's tradition of counting, via David Bernard and others) gives **c. 12,000 people in religious orders** at the eve of suppression: ~4,000 monks, ~3,000 regular canons, ~3,000 friars, ~2,000 nuns, spread across **nearly 900 religious houses** (~260 monastic, ~300 canons' houses, 142 nunneries, 183 friaries). This is stated as roughly **"one adult man in fifty"** of an assumed adult male population of ~500,000. CAVEAT: other secondary summaries give different totals for displaced religious (8,000–14,000 across various counts), reflecting different cut-off dates (1536 vs. 1540) and whether servants/lay brothers are included; treat the "1 in 50" figure as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a precise census. [Wikipedia, "Dissolution of the monasteries," accessed 2026, summarizing Knowles/Bernard-tradition figures — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries]

For **secular (parish) clergy**, J. C. Russell's classic quantitative study estimated the national ratio of regular to secular clergy at roughly 10,600:24,900 (about 30% regular), though this reconstruction is built from earlier medieval ordination and poll-tax data and should be read as a long-run medieval England estimate rather than a precise 1498–1558 figure — UNVERIFIED for exact application to this window; treat as a structural proxy. [J. C. Russell, "The Clerical Population of Medieval England," *Traditio* 2 (1944), via Cambridge Core listing — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/abs/clerical-population-of-medieval-england/1603BC8BCB8A6E340856136309761011] A diocesan-level reconstruction for York, using clergy life-expectancy modelling, puts the **secular clergy of the diocese of York at c. 4,111 around 1500** — a regional, not national, figure. [Robert Swanson-tradition study cited in "Clerical Recruitment in the Diocese of York, 1340–1530," *Journal of Ecclesiastical History*, Cambridge Core — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-ecclesiastical-history/article/abs/clerical-recruitment-in-the-diocese-of-york-13401530-data-and-commentary/DB61638191446F38BBE96DD337046837]

**Ordination age.** Canon-law norms of subdeacon at 18 and priest at 24/25 are widely cited in secondary literature on the pre-Reformation clergy (e.g., discussions built on Heath's *English Parish Clergy on the Eve of the Reformation*, 1969), but this research could not independently confirm Heath's own stated figures from an accessible source. UNVERIFIED — from secondary summary only; a later, related figure ("usual age at ordination was 23 years and six months," candidates required to be over 21) appears in a genealogical summary of Church of England ordination practice but is not clearly dated to the 1498–1558 window and may describe later Anglican practice. [FamilySearch, "Clergy of Church of England (in England)" — https://www.familysearch.org/en/wiki/Clergy_of_Church_of_England_(in_England)]

**Income (Valor Ecclesiasticus, 1535).** Henry VIII's 1535 survey — taken to assess a new 10% annual tax on clerical income — recorded roughly **8,000 parish churches, 650 monasteries, and 22 cathedrals**, plus numerous chantries, colleges, schools, hospitals, and almshouses, across England and Wales. [The National Archives / "Snapshot of Tudor England in Henry VIII's Domesday" — https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/news/snapshot-of-tudor-england-in-henry-viiis-domesday/; Henry VIII's Reign, "The Valor Ecclesiasticus" — https://www.henryviiithereign.co.uk/1535-valor-ecclesiasticus-a-valuation-of-the-church.html] The Suppression of Religious Houses Act 1535 used the survey to dissolve monasteries with declared annual income **below £200**; the survey's figures are described in Wikipedia's summary (citing Little, Platt, and Knowles) as broadly accurate but **on the low side by up to 15% in some cases**. [Wikipedia, "Valor Ecclesiasticus" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valor_Ecclesiasticus] An individual example of a modest living: the Rectory of Thorley was assessed at **£16 13s 4d per year**, illustrating the wide spread between wealthy benefices and poor parish livings referenced in the general literature (Heath 1969; Duffy 1992) — the specific poverty statistics from Heath could not be independently re-verified here and should be checked against the primary Valor text before use. [Henry VIII's Reign — https://www.henryviiithereign.co.uk/1535-valor-ecclesiasticus-a-valuation-of-the-church.html]

**Dissolution of the monasteries, 1536–1541, and pensions.** Pension arrangements for cooperating religious: **monks received roughly £5/year, nuns roughly £3/year**, and superiors (abbots/priors) were typically pensioned at about 10% of their house's net annual income; aged, infirm, or handicapped religious received more generous provision, and monastic servants were occasionally given a year's wages. Ex-nuns were treated more harshly on average and were not permitted to marry until Edward VI's reign; prioresses could be pensioned while ordinary nuns of a closed house risked destitution. English pension administration is described (in secondary literature) as more generous and administratively durable over subsequent decades than in other Protestant-reforming territories. [Wikipedia, "Dissolution of the monasteries" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries]

**Chantries, 1547–48.** The Chantries Act (Dissolution of Colleges Act) of December 1547, under Edward VI, suppressed **2,374 chantries and guild chapels** nationally, on the grounds that the doctrine of Purgatory underlying them was rejected; the Crown was statutorily bound to pension displaced chantry priests. A regional case study found **485 chantry priests** affected across the East and West Midlands alone. Chantry priests had frequently doubled as parish schoolmasters, so their loss also removed local educational and charitable provision in many villages. [Wikipedia, "Dissolution of Colleges Act 1547" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_Colleges_Act_1547; K. Farnhill (or successor scholarship), "'Of Honest Conversation and Competently Learned': The Dissolution of the Chantries (1548) and Chantry Priests of the East and West Midlands," *Midland History* 44:2 (2019), doi:10.1080/0047729X.2019.1667107 — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0047729X.2019.1667107]

**Women religious and dowries.** Nunnery entry typically required a "spiritual dowry," in principle forbidden by canon law but widely practised. At Monkton priory the customary entrance fee was **£3**, but total costs, including habit and reception-feast expenses, could run much higher: Elizabeth Sewardby's 1460 entry cost roughly **£10 7s 10½d** in total. NOTE: this specific example (1460) falls outside the 1498–1558 window and is a proxy for the general pattern, not a within-window data point; convent dowries were nonetheless understood by contemporaries and by Power's scholarship to be generally lower than comparable marriage dowries, which is why some families preferred the convent for daughters. [Eileen Power, *Medieval English Nunneries c. 1275 to 1535* (Cambridge University Press, 1922), Ch. 1, available via Project Gutenberg / Wikisource — https://www.gutenberg.org/files/39537/39537-h/39537-h.htm; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Medieval_English_nunneries_c._1275_to_1535/Chapter_I:_The_Novice]

**Reformation timeline touching the village (England).** Act of Supremacy, 1534 (Crown as Supreme Head of the Church); Dissolution of the lesser monasteries, 1536, and greater monasteries, 1538–40; Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536–37 (see Period Events); Act of Six Articles, 1539 (doctrinal conservatism under Henry VIII); first Book of Common Prayer imposed, 1549, provoking the Prayer Book Rebellion (see Period Events); Chantries Act, 1547–48; Marian religious restoration to Roman obedience, 1553–58. These dates are standard textbook chronology and are treated here as established rather than requiring individual citation beyond the specific events documented below.

#### Nobility and gentry

**Size and definition.** Lawrence Stone's *The Crisis of the Aristocracy, 1558–1641* studies the English titled peerage as a formally bounded group: **382 individual peers** held title across the period from Elizabeth I's accession (1558) to the outbreak of civil war, with a peak concurrent total of **126 peers** in the early Stuart period. CAVEAT: Stone's study window begins at 1558, i.e., just at the close of this brief's period, so it is a boundary proxy for "nobility as of 1558" rather than a mid-Tudor figure; the much larger "gentry" (untitled landed class) is not covered by this count at all. [Lawrence Stone, *The Crisis of the Aristocracy, 1558–1641* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1965); summary via Wikipedia "Lawrence Stone" and "Storm over the gentry" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Stone; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_over_the_gentry]

**Income and the "crisis" debate.** Stone's central thesis — a mid-sixteenth/seventeenth-century economic crisis of the peerage — was challenged on methodological grounds by Hugh Trevor-Roper and others; Christopher Thompson's re-analysis found the peerage's aggregate real income was **higher in 1602 than in 1534**, and grew substantially further by 1641. This matters for a simulation spanning 1498–1558: it suggests noble income was not in uniform decline across the window and that land-rent income likely rose with the price revolution for landlords able to renegotiate rents and enclose. UNVERIFIED for precise figures (percentage growth not stated in the sources found); treat as a directional finding. [Summarized via Wikipedia "Lawrence Stone" bibliography discussion — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Stone]

**Violent death.** A cross-European quantitative demographic study of titled elites found that **before 1550, roughly 30% of noble men died in battle** (i.e., violent/military death), a figure the source treats as characteristic of the late medieval "warrior aristocracy" model before a shift toward court-based, non-military elite life later in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. CAVEAT: this figure is drawn from Neil Cummins's long-run European elite lifespan dataset, not from Hollingsworth's 1964 British-peerage-specific demography as originally sought; Hollingsworth's own specific figure for "dukes' sons dying by violence in the fifteenth century" could not be verified from the sources located in this research and should be treated as UNVERIFIED until the original 1964 *Population Studies* supplement is consulted directly. [Neil Cummins, "Lifespans of the European Elite, 800–1800," *Journal of Economic History* (2017), preprint — https://neilcummins.com/Cummins2017.pdf]

**Marriage strategy and wardship.** Henry VIII formally established the **Court of Wards in 1540** (renamed Court of Wards and Liveries in 1541) to systematize and maximize Crown revenue from feudal wardship. Under wardship, when a tenant-in-chief holding land by knight service died leaving an underage heir, the Crown took custody of both the land and the heir — including control of the heir's marriage — until majority (or, for a female ward, until marriage); the Crown's claim extended to roughly **one-third of the inheritance**. The Court became one of the Tudor Crown's most lucrative fiscal institutions, and wardships/marriages were routinely bought and sold, making arranged marriage for wealth or land consolidation a structural feature of noble and gentry family strategy across this period. [Wikipedia, "Court of Wards and Liveries" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Wards_and_Liveries; UBC Wiki, "Wardship" — https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:History_344_Nasty_Families/Family_structure/Wardship]

**Education, wet-nursing, primogeniture.** Inns of Court and university attendance by gentry/noble sons, wet-nursing of infants, and strict primogeniture with cash or clerical/military careers for younger sons are well-attested structural features of the English elite family in this period in the secondary literature (Stone 1965; Mertes 1988), but this research pass did not locate independently verifiable quantitative figures (e.g., % of noble sons attending university, typical wet-nurse duration) within the time available. These should be treated as qualitative background only pending direct consultation of Stone (1965) and Mertes, *The English Noble Household 1250–1600* (1988).

#### Period events 1498–1558

- **1494–1559 — Italian Wars.** Recurrent warfare among France, Spain, the Holy Roman Empire, and Italian states across northern and central Italy; drew English, Spanish, French, and German troops and taxation demands throughout the period, indirectly touching villages across Western Europe through recruitment, taxation, and mercenary demand. (Standard chronological frame; not independently re-verified with a casualty figure in this pass.)
- **1524–1525 — German Peasants' War.** Region: south-western and central German-speaking lands of the Holy Roman Empire (Swabia, Franconia, Thuringia, Alsace, and beyond). At its height involved an estimated **300,000 peasant insurgents**; total war deaths estimated at **80,000–100,000**, mostly peasants and townsfolk, with aristocratic forces reported to have killed **up to 100,000 of the ~300,000** poorly armed insurgents. A single battle (Pfeddersheim, 23–24 June 1525) reportedly killed **8,000 peasants**. Touched: rural peasant communities and market towns; directly relevant to any village simulation set in the German lands. [Wikipedia, "German Peasants' War" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Peasants%27_War]
- **1526 — Battle of Mohács.** Ottoman victory over the Kingdom of Hungary; death of King Louis II; opened Hungary to Ottoman/Habsburg partition. (Standard chronological fact; region: Hungary; not independently re-verified with casualty figures in this pass.)
- **1529 — Siege of Vienna.** Ottoman army under Suleiman the Magnificent besieged Habsburg Vienna and was repelled; marks the furthest point of Ottoman advance into Central Europe in this period. (Standard chronological fact; region: Austria/Central Europe; not independently re-verified with casualty figures in this pass.)
- **1534 — Act of Supremacy (England).** Henry VIII declared Supreme Head of the Church of England, formally beginning the English Reformation's institutional break from Rome. Touched: every English parish, altering clerical obedience and, from 1535, tax liability (Valor Ecclesiasticus).
- **1535 — Valor Ecclesiasticus.** National survey of ecclesiastical wealth: **c. 8,000 parish churches, 650 monasteries, 22 cathedrals** recorded. Touched: every beneficed clergyman and religious house in England and Wales. [The National Archives — https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/news/snapshot-of-tudor-england-in-henry-viiis-domesday/]
- **1536–1541 — Dissolution of the Monasteries.** Nearly 900 religious houses suppressed; c. 12,000 monks, canons, friars, and nuns displaced or pensioned. Touched: every village with a monastic landlord, monastic hospital, or monastic almsgiving tradition — a substantial share of rural England, since monasteries were major landholders. [Wikipedia, "Dissolution of the monasteries" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries]
- **1536 — Vagabonds Act (poor law).** First English statute providing for structured parish collections for the poor, alongside harsher, escalating punishment for vagrancy (whipping, then ear-loss, then potential execution on a third offence). Touched: every parish, which now bore some formal responsibility for poor relief. [Wikipedia, "Vagabonds Act 1536" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vagabonds_Act_1536]
- **1536–1537 — Pilgrimage of Grace.** Region: Northern England (Yorkshire, Cumberland, Northumberland, Durham, Lancashire). A popular rising against religious and economic policy; estimates of participation range up to **~50,000**, with Robert Aske reportedly leading **9,000** followers into York and assembling **30,000–40,000** at Doncaster. **216 people were executed**, including several lords and knights, 7 abbots, 38 monks, and 16 parish priests. Touched: northern rural and market-town communities directly; the reprisals touched clergy and gentry households specifically. [Wikipedia, "Pilgrimage of Grace," citing C.S.L. Davies, "The Pilgrimage of Grace Reconsidered," *Past & Present* (1968); M.H. and R. Dodds, *The Pilgrimage of Grace 1536–1537* (1915); Claire Cross, ODNB (2009) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrimage_of_Grace]
- **1542–1551 (policy) / 1544 (public circulation) — Great Debasement.** Region: England. Silver fineness of English coinage fell from the sterling standard of **92.5%** to **50% (1545)**, **33% (1547)**, and a low of **25% (1551)**; the silver content of the penny fell by **more than 83%** overall. Officially revoked October 1551; debased coin fully withdrawn only by 1560. Touched: every villager using coined money — wages, rents, and prices were all affected by the resulting inflation ("price revolution" overlap). [Wikipedia, "Great Debasement," citing Ling-Fan Li, LSE (2009); J.D. Mackie, *The Earlier Tudors 1485–1558* (1963) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Debasement]
- **1547 — Chantries Act (Dissolution of Colleges Act).** **2,374 chantries and guild chapels** suppressed nationally. Touched: parishes that had relied on chantry priests for prayers for the dead, auxiliary pastoral care, and, frequently, schoolmastering. [Wikipedia, "Dissolution of Colleges Act 1547" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_Colleges_Act_1547]
- **1547 — Vagabonds Act.** Region: England. Introduced a harsh (and largely unenforced) enslavement clause for able-bodied vagrants; repealed 1550 after proving unworkable. Touched: rural and urban poor; symptomatic of enclosure-driven displacement. [Wikipedia, "Vagabonds Act 1547" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vagabonds_Act_1547]
- **1549 — Prayer Book Rebellion.** Region: Cornwall and Devon, 6 June–17 August 1549. Rebellion against the imposition of the first English Book of Common Prayer. Roughly **7,000 rebels** engaged; at least **2,000 rebels** and **300 government troops** killed, with one recent compiled estimate putting total deaths around **5,500**. Touched: South-Western English villages directly, both as combatants and as casualties of government reprisal. [Wikipedia, "Prayer Book Rebellion," citing Sandys-Winsch, *The Illustrated Tudor Dictionary* (2025) for the death-toll compilation — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer_Book_Rebellion]
- **1549 — Kett's Rebellion.** Region: Norfolk, 8 July–27 August 1549. Rising against enclosure and local grievances. Roughly **16,000 rebels**; at least **3,000 rebels** killed at the Battle of Dussindale, ~250 royal troops killed, and an estimated 30–300 rebels hanged in the aftermath. Touched: East Anglian villages, especially around Norwich. [Wikipedia, "Kett's Rebellion," citing Stephen K. Land, *Kett's Rebellion: The Norfolk Rising of 1549* (1977), pp. 123–125 — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kett%27s_Rebellion]
- **1553–1558 — Marian religious restoration.** Region: England. Reversal of Edwardian Reformation, restoration of Roman obedience and traditional liturgy under Mary I, accompanied by the burning of Protestant dissenters (the "Marian persecutions"). Touched: every English parish underwent a second forced liturgical reversal within a decade, following the 1549 Prayer Book imposition. (Standard chronological fact; specific execution totals not independently re-verified in this pass.)

#### Synthesis for a simulation

For a village-level life simulation set in 1498–1558, the material above suggests several concrete design parameters, each carrying the uncertainty noted above:

1. **Clergy density.** Roughly 1 beneficed/religious person per 40–50 adults is a reasonable order-of-magnitude target (derived from the "1 in 50 adult males" religious estimate, adjusted upward slightly to include secular parish clergy), but this should be treated as a tunable estimate, not a hard constant — the underlying sources disagree by a factor of ~1.5–2×.
2. **A single village priest's income should span a wide range** (Valor Ecclesiasticus shows benefices from very poor to comfortable), rather than a flat stipend — modelling income variance is more historically faithful than a single "priest salary" value.
3. **Two forced religious disruptions should be scripted as scheduled events, not random**: the Dissolution (1536–41, affecting any village with monastic landholding, almsgiving, or hospital ties) and the Chantries Act (1547–48, affecting any village with a chantry priest/schoolmaster). Both come with a pension mechanic (documented rates above) rather than simple unemployment.
4. **Marriage age for commoners should center in the mid-to-late twenties for both sexes**, not the early-teens marriages often assumed from noble/gentry wardship cases — the wardship/child-marriage pattern is a nobility-specific mechanic tied to the Court of Wards (from 1540) and should not be applied to the peasant/artisan population.
5. **Apprenticeship (age ~14, term ~7 years, ending near age 21–24) is a good universal artisan/merchant life-stage mechanic**, with guild admission and independent marriage typically following completion.
6. **Poor relief and vagrancy risk should escalate in severity across three legislative steps** (1531 licensing of the "impotent" poor → 1536 parish collections plus harsher vagrancy penalties → 1547's short-lived, harsh, and unenforced enslavement clause), giving a natural difficulty/tone curve for a simulation's social-safety mechanics across the period.
7. **Regional rebellion events are geographically specific and should not be applied nationwide**: Pilgrimage of Grace belongs to the North; Prayer Book Rebellion to the South-West; Kett's Rebellion to East Anglia; the German Peasants' War to a separate (non-English) map area. Each carries a distinct trigger (religious change; enclosure/prayer-book imposition; enclosure and rural governance grievances respectively) that could gate a "rebellion" event conditional on regional and temporal setting.
8. **Currency/price shocks (1544–51 debasement) provide a built-in economic-hardship arc** independent of harvest failure, useful for varying wage/rent/price mechanics decade-by-decade rather than holding them flat across 1498–1558.
9. Several requested figures (holy-day count per year, precise ordination ages, weaning age, specific migration percentages, Hollingsworth's ducal violent-death statistic) could **not** be verified in this pass and should either be dropped from the simulation's "hard data" layer or sourced directly from the named primary/secondary works before being hard-coded.

This section compares the current engine (`src/domain`) with the research above. The figure in the *Engine today* column is what the code does right now. The proposals below are **not decisions yet**. Once agreed, they become numbered entries in [decisions.md](./decisions.md), starting at 048.

**Biggest gap:** the actuarial table in `actuarial.ts:9-17` implies a life expectancy at birth of **about 65 years** and a **91% chance of surviving to age 15**. Wrigley & Schofield put English life expectancy at birth at **about 35 years** (range 28–42), and infant mortality alone was about 150–170 per 1,000. The illness multiplier and hardship years lower the engine's figure a little, but the table is still off by roughly a factor of two. Decision 035 tried to fix this for the protagonist only, with a per-protagonist bonus (`mortality.ts:44-54`). As a result, every other villager lives a modern lifespan.

#### i18n plan (proposal 059)

- **Routing:** `src/proxy.ts` (Next 16 renamed `middleware` to `proxy`) negotiates `Accept-Language` and redirects to `/en` or `/es`. Routes move under `src/app/[lang]/…`, and `<html lang>` is set from the route.
- **UI strings:** typed per-locale dictionaries (`src/i18n/{en,es}.ts`, about 200 strings) loaded with `getDictionary(lang)`, following the official guide (`node_modules/next/dist/docs/01-app/02-guides/internationalization.md`). No new library.
- **Narration:** already rendered from structured events at view time and never stored (`narrate.ts:21-31`), so nothing persisted needs migrating. `narrate.ts`, `vignettes.ts` and the display half of `questionText()` get a per-locale variant array at the same index. `pick()` then keeps choosing the same variant in both languages, which preserves determinism.
- **Model prompts stay in English.** `questionText()` currently feeds both the UI (`loom.tsx:360`) and the language model that makes the characters' decisions ("Jev"). It gets split into a display string and a model string, so translating the UI never changes what the model sees and the answer cache stays valid.
- **Spanish register:** neutral professional Spanish, as the artifact rules require, with period vocabulary (siervo, labrador, diezmo, gremio).
